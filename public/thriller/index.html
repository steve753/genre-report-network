YOUR MONEY: Kindle Unlimited's August Reversal — a Smaller Tracked Pool, a Higher Page Rate, and Two Figures That Do Not Agree

The Fantasy and Romance desks, and our own September issue, built a story on July 2026: a KDP Select Global Fund of $67.6 million paying a US rate of $0.004221 per page, a bigger pot paying less per page. August inverts that. Written Word Media's KDP Global Fund payouts tracker, last updated September 18, 2026 and fetched October 2, lists August 2026 at $66 million with a US per-page rate of $0.005170. The same page lists July at $67.6M and $0.004221, and June at $63.6M and $0.004615, which matches the figures the siblings and we published.

The fund moved from $67.6M to $66.0M, a drop of $1.6M or 2.37% (1.6 ÷ 67.6). The US page rate moved from $0.004221 to $0.005170, a rise of $0.000949 or 22.48% (0.000949 ÷ 0.004221). For a 400-KENP novel read to the end in the US store, that is about $2.07 in August against about $1.69 in July (400 × 0.005170 versus 400 × 0.004221). The Romance desk ran a pages-read derivation on July's figures — fund divided by rate, labeled directional only — and the same identity, rate = fund ÷ pages, works in reverse on August's. On the tracker's July and August figures, 1 − (66.0 ÷ 67.6) ÷ (0.005170 ÷ 0.004221) comes to roughly 0.20; read as pages, about a fifth fewer were read in August than in July. The number is ours, the instrument is theirs, and the estimate rests on an assumption that is not true — that the published US rate applies to the whole global pool. Amazon's own help page says otherwise: "The share of fund allocated to each country varies based on a number of factors, such as exchange rates, customer reading behavior, and local subscription pricing" (KDP Select Global Fund help page, fetched 2026-10-02). So the one-fifth is a US-rate-against-global-fund approximation, and a move in exchange rates or in the US share of the fund could account for part of it. Amazon publishes no program-wide pages-read total — the same help page says it reports pages read to each author, for that author's own titles, in the KDP reports — and neither does the tracker.

Now the conflict, which we are publishing rather than resolving. Amazon's KDP Select Global Fund help page (fetched 2026-10-02) carries the heading "Total KDP Select Author Earnings" with the figure "$69.4 Million" against August 2026. That is $3.4 million above the tracker's $66 million for the same month. The help page does not say whether its figure includes bonuses on top of the base fund. Its "Royalties" block does explain how the fund is set and divided — "We review the size of the KDP Select Global Fund each month to make it compelling for authors to enroll their Kindle eBooks in KDP Select," the fund is announced monthly in the KDP community forum, each country's share varies with exchange rates, reading behavior and local subscription pricing, authors are paid by their share of pages read, and earnings are capped at 3,000 KENPC read per title per customer — but none of it addresses the tracker's figure or defines how "Total KDP Select Author Earnings" relates to the fund. We fetched nothing that reconciles the two numbers, so both appear here with their labels, and the gap is unexplained. September's fund and rate were absent from both pages as of October 2.

If your fall forecast carried July's $0.004221 forward, August paid 22.5% more per page than that line assumed, on the tracker's figures. If your forecast assumed pages read would keep growing because the fund kept growing, our estimate says the opposite happened in August. And if you are building a spreadsheet off Amazon's own page, you are using a number $3.4 million larger than the one the trackers use for the same month, with no published statement of what the difference is.

THE CLOCK: October 31 Is the Last ITW Date for 2026 Work, and Self-Published Thrillers Qualify

The 2027 ITW Thriller Awards take submissions on deadlines staggered by publication date, and the last one falls inside this issue's shelf life. Per ITW's FAQ for Awards (fetched 2026-10-02), works published on or after August 1, 2026 must be submitted by October 31, 2026, and "No submissions will be accepted after October 31, 2026." — a date our September issue printed in passing, and this is the last issue before it. The earlier tiers have closed: works published by May 31 were due June 30, and works published by July 31 were due August 31 — the deadline our September issue covered when ITW extended it to September 6. From this issue's date there are 30 days left.

Eligibility, from the same FAQ: an eligible work is "a novel, short story, or audiobook that was published in English, for the first time, anywhere in the world, in the calendar year of 2026," and either the author is an Active ITW member or the publisher is ITW-recognized. The sentence that matters most for this readership is quoted in full: "Self-published novels, short stories, and audiobooks are eligible for the 2027 Thriller Awards provided the author is an ITW Active member." The FAQ adds that "registered copyright must be held 100% by the author or an authorized party acting on behalf of the author," and that a novel, short story or audiobook first published in any form before January 1, 2026 stays ineligible even if an ITW-recognized publisher releases it during the eligibility period — which closes the route of re-publishing an existing novel or short story through a small press to qualify it. The FAQ answers two questions that cut against that rule, and the two answers do not run the same way. One matters here: asked whether an audiobook is eligible when the print or e-book edition came out in a previous year, ITW answers "Possibly." — eligible provided the audiobook itself meets every eligibility requirement, but not if an earlier version of the book was submitted to the Thriller Awards, in any format, in a prior year. So a 2026 audiobook of an older self-published thriller is not shut out by the backlist rule, provided the book was never entered before. The other question, asked in reverse about print and e-books when only the audiobook came out in a previous year, gets an unqualified "Yes." with no prior-submission bar attached, and nothing in this issue turns on it.

Two limits on what we can tell you. We read the FAQ page only; we did not fetch a fee schedule, a submission portal, or a membership-cost page this month, so this issue states nothing about what submission or membership costs. And this is the thriller-specific award body: the Mystery desk's current issue mapped the Edgar (publisher-gated), the Anthony (open entry, attendee-gated voting) and the CWA Emerging Author Dagger, and did not cover ITW. Treat this section as the missing square on their map.

If you self-published a thriller, a short story or an audiobook on or after August 1 this year and you hold the copyright — including a new audiobook of a book you published in print or e-book in an earlier year and never entered, under the FAQ's "Possibly." answer above — you have one dated door into a genre award that the publisher-gated prizes do not offer you, and it closes on October 31. Check your membership status before the deadline rather than on it.

SALES SIGNALS: Four of Fifteen on the October 11 NYT List, by This Desk's Rule — and Why That Is Not an Increase

Four of the fifteen titles on the NYT Combined Print & E-Book Fiction list dated October 11, 2026 (sales week ending September 26) are thrillers by this desk's rule. That is our count, on our week, under our rule, and each of those three qualifiers is doing work. The rule, printed in full:

Rule A: a title counts as a thriller if both (i) the NYT's own one-line description for that title names a crime, a killing, or a criminal or hostile act, and (ii) that named act is the engine of the premise as the description states it — the thing the protagonist pursues, commits, conceals, or must survive — rather than a character's occupation, an organization's label, or background. Clause (iii): words appearing only inside the quoted title of a contained work (a short-story title, for instance) are not description content. Author reputation, publisher, and Amazon shelf placement are ignored.

Applied to all fifteen descriptions in the pack, the rule says yes to four: Vince Flynn: Double Tap at #3 ("When a scientist is kidnapped, Mitch must infiltrate a jihadi cell"), Agatha Christie: Murder at the Grand Alpine Hotel at #9 ("Miss Marple must determine the identity of a killer"), The Munich Affair at #11 ("An investigation into the death of a young woman in Hitler's apartment in 1931 puts several people at risk"), and Mad Mabel at #15 ("once Australia's youngest convicted murderer tries to keep her previous life hidden"). It says no to the other eleven, and five of those refusals deserve to be visible. Verity, this issue's lead story and #1 on Amazon's Mystery, Thriller & Suspense chart, is excluded at #8 because the NYT's description ("uncovers a horrifying truth") names no crime or act; the rule applied cold leaves it out. Sometimes I Scare Myself at #6 is excluded on clause (iii) — "Kill" appears only inside the quoted story title "Never Kill the Medium" — though clause (ii) would refuse it in any case, since the description is a list of three story titles that states no premise for the killing to be the engine of, so the count without clause (iii) is still four. Hollow Bones at #4 fails at clause (i): its description — a woman who runs an emergency-preparedness department and her husband, "a police polygraph expert," facing "a crisis in their marriage" — names no crime, killing or hostile act at all, so clause (ii) is never reached; "police polygraph expert" is an occupation, but the rule never gets as far as asking. Fallen Rook at #12 passes clause (i) and fails clause (ii): "a member of a crime organization" is a label, and the described engine is the relationship. Dungeon Crawler Carl at #10 fails at clause (i) as we read it: "are trapped in a fantasy dungeon" names a condition and a setting, not a crime, a killing, or an act the description says anyone committed — the kidnapping at #3 is a crime by name, and being trapped is not. A reader who holds that being trapped names a hostile act would pass #10 at clause (i) and, with survival as the whole of the stated premise, at clause (ii) too, for a count of five. The narrowest admission is The Munich Affair at #11, which the rule admits on "[a]n investigation into the death of a young woman" that "puts several people at risk" — a death under investigation that endangers people, which we read as a named suspected killing. A reader who holds that "death" is not "a killing" until the description says so would refuse #11, and the count under that stricter reading is three, not four. We print four, and #10 and #11 are the two calls on which it turns, one in each direction. A mechanical alternative — counting any NYT title that also appears on this pack's Amazon Mystery, Thriller & Suspense charts — yields seven of fifteen, not four: the four above plus Verity (bestseller #1), Dungeon Crawler Carl (bestseller #5) and Yesteryear (bestseller #7). We do not use it, and the reason is visible in that list: it admits a dungeon-crawl fantasy and a book-club novel, and it admits this issue's lead title, which Rule A refuses on the Times' own description. The published four depends on the rule, and we would rather you see that than take the number bare.

Three disclosures about other counts of other weeks. First, the sibling desks counted the NYT list dated September 13 (sales week ending August 29), and the Horror desk's issue states that thrillers hold three slots on that list. Our four is a different week, a different fifteen books, and a different rule; it is not an increase on their three and the two numbers cannot be compared or added. Second, our own September issue reported four of fifteen on that same September 13 list using a two-pronged method — a title counted when the author's established primary genre and the book's marketing category were both thriller, suspense or crime — which is a different rule from Rule A; this month's four is not "unchanged" from last month's four, because the two were produced differently. Third, the overlap: one of our four, Murder at the Grand Alpine Hotel, is also the Mystery desk's story, and Verity — which our rule excludes — was counted as romance-adjacent, not as romance proper, by the Romance desk on the September list; they kept it out of their strict count on the same instrument Rule A reads — the Times' own one-line description, which carries no romance language for them and names no crime for us — though theirs was the September 13 list's description of the book and ours is the October 11 list's, which adds "the basis of the movie." Eight devices, each with its rule stated and run against all nine `book_description` texts (full adjudication is in the dossier's T3 and reproduces every number here):

- Bestseller credential in the first sentence — 9 of 9 (all records). Rule: the first sentence of the description contains a bestseller credential or named list ("New York Times bestselling", "INSTANT #1 NEW YORK TIMES BESTSELLER", "Amazon Charts and Wall Street Journal bestselling series", "REESE'S BOOK CLUB PICK"). For the two records whose opener is an all-caps banner with no sentence-ending punctuation (#2 and #5), the first 140 characters were read as the opening unit; both pass on a whole-block test too. - Series or continuity framing — 4 of 9 (records 2, 4, 8, 9). Rule: the text explicitly frames the book as a sequel, prequel, numbered installment, or entry in a named ongoing series. - Velocity vocabulary — 4 of 9 (records 1, 2, 3, 8). Rule: the text contains any of gripping, propulsive, page-turner, impossible to put down, thrill ride, twist-filled, unputdownable. - Crime reaching back across decades — 3 of 9 (records 3, 7, 8). Rule: the premise links a present-day crime or investigation to a dated past event, a year or an explicit span of years ("1692"; "spans a century"; "forty-five years apart"). - Return to the family house — 2 of 9 (records 3, 6). Rule: the protagonist returns to an inherited or ancestral family property as the inciting move (Pemberton; Hale House). - Abduction as the inciting incident — 2 of 9 (records 1, 9). Rule: a specific person is kidnapped or snatched in the opening premise (the honeymooners; the scientist). - Third-party author blurbs inside the retail copy — 2 of 9 (records 2, 7). Rule: the block contains an attributed quotation from an author other than the book's own (Freida McFadden; Martha Hall Kelly, Brad Meltzer and Steve Berry). - Explicit suspect roster — 1 of 9 (record 6). Rule: the description enumerates three or more distinct people the protagonist cannot trust.

The first device is worth a paragraph. Nine of nine put a bestseller credential — a list placement, a book-club tag, or a bestselling-author tag — in the first sentence. Within that sentence, the rule for "leads with it": a record leads with the credential when the credential is read before the first clause that states what happens in the book. Eight of the nine lead by that rule, and Burton's Trust No One trails it behind the hook, opening on "[a] woman's unexpected return to her isolated family estate" and "a killer's path" before closing the sentence on "New York Times bestselling author Mary Burton." Two of the eight are the near-misses: McFadden's The Witch opens "A brand-new, haunting thriller from Freida McFadden, #1 New York Times bestselling author of The Housemaid!" and Grisham's The Law Student opens "In this original prequel to his upcoming thriller, The French Illusion, #1 New York Times bestselling author John Grisham introduces his next unforgettable hero" — in both, the credential arrives mid-sentence, after a genre tag or a series tag but before any premise, so both pass; under a stricter rule that requires the credential to be the opening words of the description, the count is six. What comes after the credential in the other eight varies: Grisham's copy follows it with a Paris honeymoon and a kidnapping; Patterson and Ellis's publisher copy, after the "Instant New York Times Bestseller!" banner and two blurbs, with a detective, a journalist and an American professor across a story that "spans a century" (the four-role roster with a resistance leader that also appears in that record is Brad Meltzer's blurb, not the publisher's copy). For an author who does not own the credential, the finding is about where the first sentence's budget goes: on this shelf, the nine books that can spend it on a credential all do, and in eight of them the credential is read before the hook is.

And three of the nine are shelved outside the genre entirely. By Rule D (a described title counts as shelved outside if its `categories` breadcrumb does not contain `Mystery, Thriller & Suspense`): Big Little Truths sits under Audible Books & Originals > Books > Literature & Fiction > Genre Fiction > Family Life, The Law Student under Kindle Store > Kindle eBooks > Literature & Fiction > Genre Fiction > Coming of Age, and The Pirate Queen under Books > Literature & Fiction > Genre Fiction > Historical > Renaissance — each printed as the full breadcrumb string, store root included — 3 of 9 outside, and 6 of 9 whose breadcrumb passes through Mystery, Thriller & Suspense > Thrillers & Suspense beneath one of three store roots (Books; Kindle Store > Books; Audible Books & Originals > Books) (pack, `descriptions[].categories`, 2026-10-01). All three rank in the top five of this genre's new-release chart. The instrument is the Mystery desk's: their current issue reads the breadcrumb on the nine highest-ranked new releases and counts how many sit outside their branch, which is exactly the sample and the field used here. The framing — what sits under the category that is not the category — is the Science Fiction desk's, who ran it on their shelf by their own read of title and content rather than by breadcrumb. Both moves are theirs; our numbers are confined to ours.
